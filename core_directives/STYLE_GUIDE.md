# ISOCBC STYLE GUIDE — "The Older Sibling" Voice (God-Mode Register)

**Status:** Core directive. Every lesson, diagram caption, margin note, quiz and activity in this
repository obeys this file. When `STYLE_GUIDE.md` and `KICD_CHECKLIST.md` disagree, **compliance wins
first, tone second** — you can be funny and still be wrong, and wrong is unforgivable.

**Scope:** KICD-aligned Grade 10–12 books (Senior School, CBC), STEM pathway. Currently Chemistry and
Biology at Grade 10.

---

## 1. Who we are talking to

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Age | 15–18 (Senior School) |
| Starting point | Bottom band — the learner KICD rubrics would score **BE / AE** (Below / Approaching Expectation) |
| Target | **ME** as the floor, **EE** as the aim (see `KICD_CHECKLIST.md` §6) |
| Reading reality | Skims. Has been told, repeatedly, that science is "not for them". Probably reads the textbook only the night before a CAT. |
| What they actually need | Momentum. Someone who believes they can move, and shows them the next step. |

**The core emotional move:** this reader is not lazy and not stupid. They are *under-translated*.
Nobody ever converted the textbook's language into theirs. That is our entire job.

> **Rule 0 — Dignity is non-negotiable.**
> "E-grade" is a **starting stat**, never an identity. We never write "slow learners", "weak students",
> "remedial", "dull", "below-average", or "these learners struggle to…". We write "you're starting from
> level 1 — here's the XP". We mock the *confusion*, never the *person*.

## 2. The voice: an older sibling, not a teacher

Picture a Form 4 in a school you trust. They are two years ahead, they already fought the exam you're
about to fight, and they're leaning on the desk explaining it in their own words — because they actually
want you to pass.

### The voice is:
- **Warm and on your side.** "We've got this one. It looks bad. It isn't."
- **Direct.** Short sentences. Active verbs. One idea per sentence.
- **Honest about difficulty.** "This part is genuinely annoying. Everyone gets stuck here. Here's the
  bit everyone gets wrong."
- **Concrete before abstract.** Real thing first, name second. (Melt sugar in a sufuria → *then* say
  "giant ionic lattice".)
- **Earnestly hype.** Momentum is a learning resource. Use it.
- **Second person, plural when struggling.** "You", "we", "us". Never "the learner should be able to…"
  in learner-facing text (that phrasing belongs in the teacher-facing metadata only).

### The voice is NOT:
- Corporate / administrative ("It is important to note that…", "In this chapter we shall consider…")
- Patronising ("Now boys and girls, isn't this fun?")
- Cringe-forced. If a joke has to be explained, cut it.
- A narrating textbook with jokes bolted on.

## 3. "God-Mode" — what the term means here

**"God-Mode" is internal shorthand for the learner-facing power register.** It is a *narrative frame*,
not a claim about the software, and not a claim that the student is cheating.

The frame: the learner is a player who has been handed the strategy guide. Every concept is an
**unlock**. Every hard sub-strand is a **boss**. Every misconception is a **trap the game set**.
Knowledge is **stats**. Practice is **grinding XP** (said lightly — grind culture is not aspirational).

### Approved vocabulary (use with restraint — 2–4 instances per lesson, not per paragraph)
`unlock` · `boss fight` · `side quest` · `cheat code` · `XP` · `level up` · `loadout` · `patch notes`
· `speedrun` · `hack` (as in *"the hack for remembering this"*, never *"hack the exam"*) · `stats`
· `respawn` · `final boss` · `the whole map`

### Banned vocabulary (God-Mode overuse)
`god mode` / `godmode` **written in learner-facing text** (too cringe, too meta, and it reads like
cheating) · `NPC` (used to describe real people) · `sigma` · `grind` as a moral command · `noob` ·
`clapped` · anything implying another student is worthless.

### Hard boundary — the two things we never gamify
1. **Laboratory and chemical safety.** When the text touches safety (acids, chlorine gas, heating,
   glassware, dissection, fieldwork), the tone flips to **flat, literal, unambiguous**. No joke, no
   metaphor, no "hack" within three sentences of a safety instruction. Safety lines get their own
   `> ⚠ SAFETY` callout and are never inside a joke block.
2. **Academic integrity.** We teach *how to pass*, never how to cheat. No "shortcut" framing around
   copying, smuggling material, or impersonation. Speedrun = study efficiently. It never means bypass.

## 4. The Translation Protocol (KLB → God-Mode)

Textbook prose (KLB and friends) is dense, nominalised, and front-loads the abstract. Convert in four
passes. **Never publish a sentence that has not been through all four.**

| Pass | Name | Action | Test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Strip** | Remove every clause that does not change what the student must *do*. Delete hedges, "it should be noted", stacked prepositional phrases, triple-noun stacks. | Can a student read it aloud without running out of breath? |
| 2 | **Anchor** | Put a real, physically-possible thing in the sentence. Kenyan and local where honest: jiko, sufuria, posho mill, boda boda, tea farm, Rift Valley soil, Nairobi traffic, maize flour, charcoal. | Is there a noun you could point at? |
| 3 | **Say it plain** | Write the meaning as you'd say it to a friend at the gate. No jargon at all. This is the *plain line*. | Would a 12-year-old get the gist? |
| 4a | **Name it** | Only *after* the plain line, hand over the exam word, highlighted as a term: **`electrostatic attraction`**. | Does the student now own the vocabulary the exam will use? |
| 4b | **Trap it** | Name the single most common wrong answer and kill it explicitly. | Have we pre-empted the mistake? |

### Worked example — Chemistry, bonding

> **KLB source:** "Giant ionic structures are characterised by high melting and boiling points owing to
> the presence of strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions which extend
> throughout the lattice, necessitating considerable energy to overcome."

**Strip:** Ionic compounds melt at high temperatures because the attraction between the ions is strong
and runs through the whole structure, so it takes a lot of energy to break.

**Anchor + say it plain:**
> Imagine a crate of soda bottles welded together in a block. To get one bottle out, you don't just lift
> it — you have to break the whole block apart. Table salt is like that. Every positive sodium is
> gripping every negative chloride around it, in every direction, all the way through the crystal. That's
> why salt melts at 801 °C. Your sufuria will give up long before the salt does.

**Name it (Pass 4a):**
> The exam word for that grip is **strong electrostatic forces of attraction**, and the block is a
> **giant ionic lattice**. Learn those two phrases — they are worth marks every single year.

**Trap it (Pass 4b):**
> ⚠ **Trap:** people write "salt has strong bonds between molecules." Salt has **no molecules**. Write
> "between oppositely charged ions". One word difference, one mark gone.

## 5. Mechanics of the page

### Sentence and paragraph rhythm
- Average sentence: **12–18 words.** Hard ceiling: 30. If it exceeds 30, split it.
- Paragraph: **1–4 sentences.** Never a wall of text.
- Open sections with a question, a scene, or a stakes statement. Never with a definition.
- Vary rhythm deliberately: short punch after a longer explanation.

### Every concept block uses this spine
1. **Hook** — why this matters, or a question the student already cares about. (1–2 sentences.)
2. **Plain line** — the idea with zero jargon.
3. **The picture** — diagram / mental model / analogy. (See `assets/diagrams/README.md`.)
4. **The exam word** — the terminology, bolded, with pronunciation help if it's a mouthful.
5. **Worked move** — one fully-solved example, every step shown, no skipped arithmetic.
6. **Trap** — the classic wrong answer, named and killed.
7. **Rep** — 3–6 questions, ramping difficulty. Answers at the back of the chapter, with reasoning.

### Formatting rules
- Bold a term **only on first use** in a chapter. Later uses are plain.
- Use `⚠` for traps and safety, `💡` for optional depth, `🎮` for the God-Mode aside, `✅` for checkpoints.
- Diagrams: every diagram gets a caption **and** a one-line "what to notice". Never an unlabelled figure.
- Use tables for comparisons, never for narrative.
- Equations: always state in words what the equation *means* immediately after it appears.
- Numerals for measurements and quantities ("3 moles", "801 °C"); spell out one to nine in prose.
- Kenyan English conventions: **-ise** (organise, not organize) is preferred; dates as *14 March 2026*;
  currency as *KSh 250*.

### Local flavour — use sparingly
A little Kenyan register makes the book feel like it was written here, because it was. Maximum
**1–2 local words per page**, never in an exam-critical sentence, never translated condescendingly.
Examples: *sufuria, jiko, boda boda, chai, mandazi, mzito, msee, sasa, pole, harambee, shamba, mkate*.
> ⚠ **Editor's note:** this lexicon must be reviewed by a Kenyan-born editor before print. Sheng moves
> fast and ages badly. When in doubt, use plain, warm English — that never dates.

### Accessibility
- Define **all** abbreviations on first use (CBC, KICD, KNEC, CBA, PCI, IUPAC).
- Alt-text for every diagram (see `assets/diagrams/README.md`).
- Never encode meaning in colour alone; pair colour with a label, pattern, or shape.
- Keep the text fully comprehensible if all diagrams and callouts are removed.

## 6. Banned list — do not ship these

**Dead textbook filler:** "It should be noted that" · "It is worth mentioning" · "In this chapter we
shall" · "As we have seen" · "It can therefore be concluded that" · "The learner should be able to"
(in learner-facing prose) · "Various" · "A number of" · "Utilise" (use *use*) · "Facilitate" ·
"In order to" (use *to*) · "Commence" (use *start*) · "Numerous" (use *many*).

**Demoralising:** "as should be obvious" · "clearly" · "simply" · "obviously" · "of course" ·
"it is easy to see" · "any student should know" · "as we learned in primary school" (unless the book
*then re-teaches it* — and it usually should).

**Passive-voice dodge:** "mistakes were made", "the solution is then heated". In instructions, always
use imperative: "Heat the solution." In explanation, prefer active: "The acid eats the magnesium",
not "the magnesium is consumed by the acid".

## 7. Tone calibration dial

| Context | Register |
| --- | --- |
| Explaining a hard concept | Warm sibling, maximum concrete, hype allowed |
| Worked example / calculation | Calm, precise, every step shown, minimal jokes |
| Exam strategy / marking schemes | Coach. Blunt, tactical, mark-focused. |
| Laboratory / chemical / field safety | Flat, literal, imperative. **Zero jokes.** |
| Traps and common mistakes | Playful-tough. "The exam set this trap. Don't walk into it." |
| Careers and real-life links | Straight-talking and encouraging, never cheerleading |
| Anything about the student's past results | Never referenced except to say it's a starting point |

## 8. Pre-ship checklist for any page

- [ ] Passed all four passes of the Translation Protocol
- [ ] Opens with a hook, not a definition
- [ ] Every jargon term introduced *after* its plain meaning
- [ ] At least one Kenyan/physical anchor
- [ ] At least one named trap
- [ ] No sentence over 30 words; no paragraph over 4 sentences
- [ ] No banned phrase from §6
- [ ] Safety instructions (if any) are flat, literal, in their own callout
- [ ] Matches the KICD strand / sub-strand / outcomes it claims to teach (`KICD_CHECKLIST.md`)
- [ ] Read aloud once. If it sounds like a textbook, rewrite it.
